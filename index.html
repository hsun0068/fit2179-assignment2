Australia Health & Lifestyle

Introduction

Australia is often imagined as active, outdoorsy and health-conscious. However, health data suggests a more complex story. Weight-related risk has changed over time, varies across states and territories, differs between population groups, and is linked with lifestyle behaviours such as inactivity and sedentary time.

Act 1

Are Australians really healthy? Tracking overweight and obesity across states

Reading guide: Select a survey year to compare changes across Australian states and territories. The map gives the spatial pattern, while the ranking chart shows which states sit higher or lower in that year.

Adult overweight and obesity rates have risen across most Australian states and territories from 2007–08 to 2022. The increase is not identical everywhere: Queensland shows the largest long-term rise, Tasmania remains consistently high, and several states reached their highest point around 2017–18 before easing slightly in 2022.

Overweight and Obesity Rates Across Australian States (2007–2022)

Tasmania stays among the highest-rate states throughout the period, reaching above 70% in 2017–18 and 2022. Queensland becomes more prominent over time, rising from 60.8% in 2007–08 to 68.8% in 2022.

Sources: Australian Bureau of Statistics - National Health Survey 2022; ABS - National Health Survey: State and territory findings 2022; ABS - National Health Survey: State and territory findings 2017-18
ABS - Australian Statistical Geography Standard (ASGS) Edition 3 digital boundary files

State Ranking by Overweight and Obesity Rate

In 2022, Tasmania records the highest rate at 70.5%, followed by Queensland at 68.8%. New South Wales records the lowest value at 63.1%, creating a 7.4 percentage-point gap between the highest and lowest states.

Sources: Australian Bureau of Statistics - National Health Survey 2022
ABS - National Health Survey: State and territory findings 2022; ABS - National Health Survey: State and territory findings 2017-18

Takeaway: Overall, adult overweight and obesity rates have increased across Australia from 2007–08 to 2022. Although some states eased slightly after their 2017–18 peak, the long-term trend is clearly upward. Now, let’s look at which groups of people are most likely to be affected.
Act 2

Who is Most Affected by Overweight and Obesity?

Reading guide: Read the three charts from top to bottom. First, the BMI composition chart gives an overview of population groups. Then, the age and gender profile shows where risk becomes stronger. Finally, the gender gap chart focuses directly on the difference between male and female rates.

Act 1 showed that overweight and obesity have increased across Australian states and territories. Act 2 shifts the focus from place to people. The question is no longer only where the rates are higher, but who is more likely to be affected.

BMI Composition Across Australian Population Groups

The BMI composition chart gives an overview of how weight categories are distributed across different population groups. It shows that overweight and obesity become more visible in older age groups, while younger adults have a larger healthy-weight share.

Source: Australian Bureau of Statistics - Waist circumference and BMI, 2022

Age and Gender Risk Profile

The heatmap makes the age pattern clearer. Overweight and obesity rates rise from young adulthood into middle and older age groups. The darker cells show that weight-related risk becomes stronger as age increases.

Source: Australian Bureau of Statistics - Waist circumference and BMI, 2022

Gender Gap in Overweight and Obesity

The gender gap chart focuses directly on the difference between male and female rates. It highlights that males often record higher overweight and obesity rates than females, especially in several adult age groups.

Source: Australian Bureau of Statistics - Waist circumference and BMI, 2022

Takeaway: Overweight and obesity are not evenly spread across the Australian population. The risk becomes stronger in older age groups and is often higher among males. This means Australia’s obesity problem is not only about where people live, but also about who they are.
Act 3

Does Inactivity Help Explain Obesity Risk?

Reading guide: Start with the scatter plot to compare physical inactivity and overweight or obesity across states. Then use the sedentary-time dashboard to look more closely at how BMI groups overlap with daily sitting time.

After identifying where overweight and obesity are higher and who is more affected, Act 3 looks at lifestyle behaviour. The aim is not to prove causation, but to see whether physical inactivity and sedentary routines appear alongside higher BMI risk.

Physical Inactivity and Obesity Move Together

The scatter plot compares physical inactivity with overweight and obesity across Australian states and territories. States further to the right have higher physical inactivity, while states higher on the chart have higher overweight or obesity rates. The upward pattern suggests that inactive routines may be part of the broader obesity risk story.

Sources: Australian Bureau of Statistics - National Health Survey 2022
ABS - National Health Survey: State and territory findings 2022

Where Do BMI and Sedentary Time Overlap?

The sedentary-time dashboard shifts from state-level comparison to participant-level distribution. The heatmap shows where BMI groups and sedentary-time ranges overlap. Darker cells indicate more participants, helping reveal whether higher BMI groups are also associated with longer sitting time.

Source: Australian Bureau of Statistics - National Health Survey 2022

Takeaway: Inactivity is one of the clearest lifestyle signals in this story. At the state level, higher physical inactivity often appears alongside higher overweight and obesity rates. At the participant level, sedentary-time patterns add a second layer, showing how daily routines may contribute to broader BMI risk.
Act 4

How Does Winter Add Extra Health Pressure?

Reading guide: Follow the monthly pattern to identify when influenza pressure rises. This section does not claim that flu causes obesity; instead, it adds a seasonal layer to the wider health story.

Health behaviour does not happen in isolation. Seasonal illness can disrupt daily routines, reduce outdoor activity and place extra pressure on individuals and the health system. Act 4 adds this seasonal context to the lifestyle story.

Influenza Records Rise During the Colder Months

The flu chart shows how influenza-related records change across the year. When illness pressure rises, maintaining active and healthy routines can become more difficult. This makes winter an important background condition in the broader health-risk story.

Sources: Australian Centre for Disease Control - NNDSS public dataset - influenza (laboratory confirmed)
ABS - Deaths due to acute respiratory infections in Australia

Takeaway: Winter adds another layer to Australia’s health story. Seasonal illness does not cause obesity, but it can place extra pressure on routines that are already difficult to maintain, especially physical activity and time spent outdoors.
Act 5

How Do Multiple Risks Combine Across States?

Reading guide: The parallel profile chart compares several health indicators at once. The final quadrant chart then separates states that are already high from states that have increased more quickly over time.

The final act brings the separate parts of the story together. Overweight and obesity are important, but they are not the only health risks. By comparing several indicators together, we can see whether some states face a broader combined burden.

Health Risk Profiles Across Australian States

Each line represents one Australian state or territory. Each indicator is normalised from low to high risk, so a higher position means that state ranks higher for that risk compared with other states. This view shows whether some states remain high across multiple indicators, rather than only one.

Sources: Australian Bureau of Statistics - National Health Survey 2022
ABS - National Health Survey: State and territory findings 2022

High Now or Rising Fast?

A high current rate and a fast increase do not mean the same thing. This final chart separates states that are already high from states that have increased more quickly over time. This helps distinguish persistent burden from emerging risk.

Sources: Australian Bureau of Statistics - National Health Survey 2022
ABS - National Health Survey: State and territory findings 2022; ABS - National Health Survey: State and territory findings 2017-18

Final takeaway: Australia’s health challenge is not one-dimensional. Overweight and obesity vary by state, age, gender, lifestyle behaviour and seasonal pressure. Some places need immediate attention because current rates are high, while others need earlier prevention because the increase has been faster.
